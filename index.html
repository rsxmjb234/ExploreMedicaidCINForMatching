Meet Maria

She's 26. She lives in upstate New York. And she needs help.

She's excited

Maria just found out she's pregnant. She's nervous and hopeful and ready to start prenatal care.

She's concerned

Maria is HIV-positive. She knows this makes her pregnancy higher-risk and she wants specialized care to protect her baby.

Maria is on Medicaid — and Medicaid wants to help her

New York Medicaid has specific programs for pregnant members who are HIV-positive: coordinated prenatal care, antiretroviral management, prevention of mother-to-child transmission. Maria qualifies. Maria wants these services. Medicaid wants to provide them.

Three parties. None can find each other.

Maria, Medicaid, and NYeC could solve this together — but the data doesn't connect them.

The MPI cannot link these three records. Different addresses (Albany, Utica, Whitesborough). Different MRNs (1234, 34432432, 2132). A name change (Alvarez to Rodriguez). And no CIN in the HIV or pregnancy feeds to tie them together. Three identities. One real Maria. Zero connection.

Maria falls through the cracks.

Medicaid's outreach program queries: "Who is enrolled, HIV-positive, AND pregnant?" Maria should appear. She doesn't. No outreach fires. No care coordination begins. She's on her own.

One field fixes everything.

If the HIV feed and the pregnancy feed had included Maria's CIN, the MPI would have linked all three records instantly.

One CIN. One Maria. Instant match. CIN is deterministic — it matches or it doesn't. No scoring, no thresholds, no ambiguity. Despite different names, addresses, MRNs, and providers, CIN links them all. The outreach program finds Maria. Care coordination begins.

The ask is simple.

Include Medicaid CIN in every health data feed that might contain a Medicaid member. Providers already know the CIN — they verify eligibility before billing. It just needs to flow into the reporting feeds.

One field. One link. One Maria.
