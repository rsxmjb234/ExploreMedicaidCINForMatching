Meet Maria

She's 26. She lives in upstate New York. She knows she needs help. She has no idea help is available.

She's excited

Maria just found out she's pregnant. She's nervous and hopeful and ready to start prenatal care.

She's concerned

Maria is HIV-positive. She knows this makes her pregnancy higher-risk. She wants specialized care to protect her baby — she just doesn't know where to find it or that anyone is already looking out for her.

Maria is on Medicaid — and Medicaid wants to help her

New York Medicaid has specific programs for pregnant members who are HIV-positive: coordinated prenatal care, antiretroviral management, prevention of mother-to-child transmission. Maria qualifies. Maria wants these services. Medicaid wants to provide them. Maria just doesn't know any of this exists — she's never been told, and nobody has reached out.

Meanwhile, the SHIN-NY already knows

When Maria's PCP documents her pregnancy, that clinical data flows into the SHIN-NY almost immediately. This is something claims data can't do — claims can lag weeks or months behind the actual visit. The SHIN-NY sees Maria's pregnancy in near real time.

The SHIN-NY exists to benefit Medicaid. It wants to get Maria's information to the people who can help her — the care coordination team that runs the prenatal HIV program. But right now, the SHIN-NY has no way to tell that team "this pregnant patient is also a Medicaid member who is HIV-positive." That's the missing link.

Maria moved twice and changed her name. Without CIN in the feeds, the MPI can't link her records.

Maria falls through the cracks.

Medicaid's outreach program queries: "Who is enrolled, HIV-positive, AND pregnant?" Maria should appear. She doesn't. No outreach fires. No care coordination begins. Maria is still waiting for help she doesn't know exists.

What If Two People Have the Same CIN?

A fair question. If we're adding CIN to matching, what happens when two records share the same CIN — through a typo, a system error, or some future edge case? The answer: the MPI handles it the same way it handles every other shared field. Because this already happens all the time.

The MPI already deals with duplicate field values every day

The MPI constantly receives records where two different people share the same value in a field:

FieldDo two people ever share the same value?Does the MPI merge them?
Date of BirthYes — constantly. Millions of NYers share a DOB.No — other fields differ
AddressYes — roommates, families, apartmentsNo — other fields differ
First NameYes — extremely commonNo — other fields differ
Phone NumberYes — shared phones, old numbers recycledNo — other fields differ
CINShouldn't happen — but if it does?No — same logic applies

CIN is treated like any other demographic field. It contributes to the match score. It doesn't override everything else. One matching field among many mismatches = no link.

Through a data entry error, Tom Jones was assigned the same CIN as Maria: A123B456. Here are their records side by side:

What Medicaid has told us about CIN

Medicaid has confirmed:

  • They never reuse CIN — once assigned, it's that person's for life
  • They don't assign one CIN to multiple people (e.g., no shared mom/baby CIN)

That said, we're ready for typos or if that policy ever changes. The MPI treats CIN as one signal among many — it doesn't blindly merge on CIN alone.

Think of CIN like a Date of Birth

DOB is one of the most powerful fields in matching. But it's not gospel — we don't merge two people just because they share a birthday. CIN works the same way:

Scenario What the MPI does Outcome
Same CIN, very different demographics (Tom vs. Maria) Scores the pair — 1 match, 7 mismatches Correctly kept apart
Same DOB, very different demographics Scores the pair — 1 match, many mismatches Correctly kept apart
Same CIN + same name + same DOB, different address Scores the pair — many matches, 1 mismatch Correctly linked (same person moved)
No CIN, different address, same person Not enough matching fields to be confident Missed match — person is fragmented
CIN is a powerful fact that helps connect data — but it's not a simple truth that is gospel. It contributes to matching like DOB, address, or name. It makes the MPI better at linking records that belong together, without creating false links when records don't.

The real risk isn't a duplicate CIN.

A duplicate CIN (typo, error) is something the MPI handles naturally — the same way it handles two people with the same birthday. The real risk is missing CIN, which causes the MPI to fragment real people into multiple identities. That's what hurts real patients like Maria.

The Ask

We're asking two things of QEs.

Why we're asking

Because we are fundamentally an extension of Medicaid, and as such we are obligated to do all that we can to help that specific population.

Because we are mission-driven organizations that want to help Medicaid succeed.

And because this ask lines up directly with the missions our own QEs and NYeC have already committed to:

  • Because NYeC's mission is to improve health care through the exchange of health information whenever and wherever needed — a Medicaid member's CIN is exactly the kind of information that needs to move wherever she goes.
    — NYeC / SHIN-NY 2020 Roadmap
  • Because Bronx RHIO's mission is to make it possible for a patient's medical records to follow them wherever they go for care — that's precisely what fails when Maria moves three times and CIN isn't there to follow her.
    — Bronx RHIO mission, GuideStar
  • Because Healthix's mission is mobilizing health information across communities to advance patient care — and Healthix has already used its data to help retain HIV-positive individuals in care. Adding CIN extends that same work to Medicaid members like Maria.
    — Healthix, Who We Are
  • Because HealtheConnections is committed to supporting better care, better outcomes, and healthier communities through trusted data exchange — connecting a member's roster status to her clinical data is trusted data exchange doing its job.
    — HealtheConnections, A Message to Our Network
  • Because HEALTHeLINK's mission includes improving health outcomes and advancing health equity in Western New York — equity means a Medicaid member shouldn't fall through the cracks just because she moved or changed her name.
    — HEALTHeLINK, Mission & History
1

Get Medicaid CIN into your infrastructure

On as many feeds as possible, from as diverse a set of sources as possible, capture the Medicaid CIN and bring it into your QE's systems. Labs, PCP visits, ADTs, CCDs, immunizations — anywhere a Medicaid member shows up.

If you already have it — great! The more feeds that carry CIN, the stronger the matching becomes across the network.

2

Convey those CINs (along with MRNs) to the NYeC Verato MPI

Send the CIN alongside your existing MRN data when you sync with the statewide Verato MPI. The mechanism depends on your QE — file submission, API call, or Verato-to-Verato sync — but the goal is the same: get CIN into the statewide identity layer.

What about the local MPI?

Whether you also add CIN to your local matching algorithm is a local decision. We are not giving direction on the local MPI. Our ask is specifically about getting CIN to the NYeC statewide Verato MPI so the SHIN-NY can link records across QEs.

Two asks. One goal.

Get CIN into your infrastructure from as many sources as possible. Then pass it to the statewide MPI. Because Maria — and everyone like her — deserves to be seen as one person by the systems built to help her.

Is the Member Roster Enough?

Why do we need to add CIN to clinical data?

A reasonable question: "We already load the Medicaid Member Eligibility File (MEF) into the MPI. The MEF has CIN. Isn't that enough?" The answer is no — because the MEF only tells us who is enrolled. It doesn't tell us anything about their health.

The core question:

"Of all people on the Medicaid member roster, who is pregnant AND has an HIV diagnosis?"

The MEF can tell us the first part (who's on the roster). But pregnancy and HIV come from clinical data — labs, PCP visits, clinical documents. That data lives in completely different systems.

What the MEF gives us (and what it doesn't)

FieldIn the MEF?Why it matters

The MEF has CIN — but it has no clinical data. The clinical data (labs, diagnoses, pregnancy) lives in separate feeds from different providers. Those feeds do NOT have CIN today. So even though the MEF links a CIN to a person, we can't link that person's clinical events back to their CIN.

The gap: clinical data doesn't carry CIN

Here's the disconnect. The MEF knows Maria is a Medicaid member (CIN A123B456). But the lab that reported her HIV result and the PCP that documented her pregnancy didn't include CIN in their data. So the SHIN-NY can't connect them.

Why we need CIN in the clinical data — not just in the MEF

The MEF already has CIN. But that only helps if the clinical feeds also carry CIN. Without CIN in the lab feed and the PCP feed, the MPI has no reliable way to say "this lab result belongs to this Medicaid member."

Think of it this way:

  • MEF record: "Maria Alvarez, CIN A123B456, is on Medicaid" — great, we know she's enrolled.
  • Lab result (no CIN): "Maria Alvarez, 126 Cross Street, Utica — HIV positive" — is this the same Maria? The MPI isn't sure. Different address, different MRN, no CIN to confirm.
  • Lab result (with CIN): "Maria Alvarez, CIN A123B456, 126 Cross Street, Utica — HIV positive" — instant match. Same CIN = same person. Done.

CIN in the MEF tells us who's a member. CIN in the clinical data tells us which clinical events belong to that member. You need both sides of the connection.

The status quo doesn't work

Approach What it gives us What's missing
Just load the MEF List of Medicaid members No clinical data — can't identify who is pregnant or HIV+
MEF + lab feeds (no CIN in labs) Members in one system, labs in another Can't reliably link members to their lab results when they move

The roster tells us who. The clinical data tells us what. CIN connects them.

The MEF is necessary — it identifies Medicaid members. But it's not sufficient. To answer questions like "who is pregnant and HIV-positive?", we need clinical data from labs and PCPs. And to connect that clinical data to the right member, those feeds need to carry CIN. Without CIN in the clinical feeds, the roster is just a list of names with no health context attached.
